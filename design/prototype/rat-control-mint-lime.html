Rat control, diagnosed properly before anything is treated

Rats are one of the most commonly reported pests in UK homes and businesses, and also one of the most misunderstood. This page covers what to look for, why it's worth taking seriously, what a professional visit involves, and how to stop it happening again.

What professional rat control involves

Professional rat control isn't a single action — it's a short sequence of decisions, each of which depends on what's actually found at the property. That starts with confirming there's genuine rat activity (as opposed to mice, or an old sign that's no longer current), understanding how widespread it is, and identifying how rats are most likely getting in. Only once that picture is clear does treatment get chosen, because the right approach for a single rat spotted in a garden shed is different from the right approach for an established population in a loft void.

This is also why response-time and price shouldn't really be quoted before any of that is known — anyone offering a fixed price for “rat control” without asking a single question about the property is guessing. A proper assessment takes that guesswork out, which is both more accurate and, in most cases, faster to resolve overall because the right method gets used from the start.

Rats are resilient, adaptable animals that have lived alongside humans for thousands of years. Treating an infestation isn't a single clever trick — it's an accurate diagnosis plus consistent follow-through.

Signs of a rat infestation

Rats are largely nocturnal and naturally cautious, so direct sightings during the day are actually one of the less common ways an infestation is first noticed. These are the signs worth paying attention to:

Droppings Dark, roughly rice-grain-sized, often clustered near food sources, along skirting boards, or inside cupboards. Fresh droppings are soft and moist; older ones are dry and crumble easily.
Gnaw marks On skirting, door frames, stored food packaging, and — significantly — electrical cabling, since a rat's teeth grow continuously and gnawing is instinctive rather than optional for them.
Scratching or movement sounds Most commonly heard at night, in wall cavities, under floors, or in lofts, as rats are most active after dark and tend to travel along the same routes repeatedly.
Grease marks and smudges Dark, greasy marks along skirting boards or beams, left by a rat's fur brushing against the same surface repeatedly on a regular travel route.
Burrows outdoors Smooth-sided holes roughly 6–9cm across, often near decking, compost heaps, garden sheds, or the base of fences, usually with a well-worn path leading to and from the entrance.
An unusual smell A persistent, slightly musky or ammonia-like odour in an enclosed space — often the first thing noticed in a loft or under-stairs cupboard before any visual sign is found.

One sign on its own — a single dropping, one distant sound at night — isn't necessarily cause for alarm. What generally does warrant a closer look is two or more of these signs appearing in the same area within a short period, since that's more consistent with ongoing activity than a one-off.

Why rat activity is a serious problem

It's tempting to think of rats as simply unpleasant rather than genuinely risky, but there are three concrete reasons professional advice consistently treats rat activity as worth addressing promptly rather than monitoring.

RISK 01 Health Rats can carry a number of pathogens transmissible to humans, including Weil's disease (leptospirosis), salmonella, and hantavirus, most commonly through contact with urine, droppings, or contaminated surfaces rather than through a bite. This is part of why food-preparation areas and stored food are a particular concern when rats have been active nearby.
RISK 02 Property damage As covered above, a rat's need to gnaw continuously means wiring, pipework, insulation, and structural timber are all at risk. Damaged electrical cabling specifically has been documented as a contributing factor in property fires, which elevates this from a maintenance issue to a genuine safety consideration.
RISK 03 Rate of increase Because rats breed quickly and reach maturity within weeks, a small, easily resolved issue can become a substantially larger one in a matter of months if left unaddressed — which is the single biggest argument for acting on early signs rather than a “wait and see” approach.

Why rats enter a property

Rats don't target a property at random — they respond to three things: access, food, and shelter. Understanding which of these is present is usually more useful than simply knowing “there are rats nearby”, because it points directly at what needs to change.

Gaps and access points Rats can fit through gaps as small as 15–20mm (roughly the width of a thumb), so unsealed pipework entries, damaged airbricks, gaps under doors, and worn mortar joints are all realistic entry routes, not just obvious holes.
Accessible food This includes the obvious (open bins, pet food left out overnight, bird feeders with heavy spillage) and the less obvious, like compost heaps with food waste, or fruit fallen from garden trees left uncollected.
Shelter and warmth Cluttered sheds, dense ivy or overgrown garden boundaries, cavity walls, and insulated lofts all provide the kind of undisturbed, sheltered space rats look for, particularly as outdoor temperatures fall.
Nearby infrastructure Proximity to drains, sewers, or waterways can bring rats into a wider area regardless of what an individual property does, which is one reason neighbouring properties sometimes experience activity around the same time.

This is also why a visit focused only on removing the rats currently present, without addressing why they arrived, tends to see the problem recur — often within the same property, sometimes within weeks.

DIY traps vs. professional treatment

Shop-bought traps and bait stations aren't ineffective, and for a single, freshly noticed rat in a garden, they're often a reasonable first step. Where they tend to fall short is with an established or growing population — this is where the practical differences matter:

A reasonable rule of thumb: if a single trap resolves the sighting within a few days and nothing recurs, that may be all that was needed. If activity continues, spreads to a new area, or comes back after a quiet spell, that's usually the point where a proper assessment saves more time than it costs.

What affects the scope of the job

Every rat control visit is shaped by the same handful of variables. Knowing them in advance helps set realistic expectations before an assessment even happens:

Property type A terraced house sharing party walls with neighbouring properties raises different considerations than a detached house or a commercial unit — shared structures mean activity can move between properties.
Extent of activity An isolated sighting is a different job to signs across multiple rooms or an established nest — this is precisely what the assessment is for.
Access Loft hatches, external access to voids, and whether affected areas are in communal or private space all affect how straightforward treatment is.
Construction Older buildings with suspended timber floors or cavity walls often have more potential rat routes than modern solid-floor construction.
Time of year Autumn and winter tend to see higher indoor activity as rats seek shelter from the cold, which can mean slightly longer resolution timelines during peak season purely due to the volume of enquiries.

Prevention and long-term protection

Prevention is genuinely more effective — and cheaper — than repeated treatment, and most of it comes down to removing the three things covered earlier: access, food, and shelter.

Sealing access points is the single highest-impact step. This includes fitting wire mesh over airbrick openings, sealing gaps where pipework enters the building, repairing damaged mortar, and fitting a door sweep where there's a visible gap under external doors.

Managing food sources means storing pet food in sealed containers rather than bags, using a bin with a well-fitting lid, clearing fallen fruit promptly, and being mindful of bird feeder spillage, which is a more common attractant than most people expect.

Reducing shelter means keeping garden vegetation, log piles, and stored materials away from the base of walls and fences, and periodically checking loft and shed spaces for early signs rather than only after activity is already suspected.

None of this guarantees rats will never approach a property again — they're widespread and highly adaptable animals — but it substantially reduces both the likelihood and the speed at which any new activity could establish itself.
